Chapter 2 — The Debt Collector

The smell of fried tilapia and burnt palm oil seeped through my floorboards like a persistent ghost. It was the sort of smell that got into your clothes, your hair, the soft tissue of your memories. I'd stopped noticing it three years ago, which was worse than noticing. You lose the texture of things, living above a noodle stall that never closes. The city outside my window hummed with the frequency of a bad tooth.

Rain sheeted down the reinforced glass in curtains of static.

The client sat across from me in the chair that wobbled unless you knew the exact angle to lean back. He didn't know the angle. He perched on the edge like a bird afraid of its own shadow, rain dripping from the hood of a coat that cost more than my rent. The coat was dry-weave, the kind that repels everything. Yet he shook. The wetness on his face wasn't rain.

"I need you to look at this," he said.

His voice had the nasal quality of someone who'd grown up in the upper stacks, where the air got thin and the acoustics bounced off glass. He held a data-slate like it might bite him.

"I'm not a courier service," I said. "If you want message drops, go to the pigeon man on Level 4. He charges by the gram and doesn't ask questions."

"This isn't a message." He slid the slate across my desk. The surface was scratched, the screen cracked in a spiderweb pattern from some impact. "It's a memory. Encrypted. I need you to decrypt it and tell me what it contains."

I lit a cigarette from the pack I kept in my desk drawer, the one with the faded label that promised something called "Menthol Paradise" that definitely was not paradise and probably wasn't menthol either. The smoke curled toward the ceiling fan, which spun with a lazy wobble.

"Memories aren't my trade anymore," I said. "I'm out of that business."

"That's not what I heard."

"Then you heard wrong."

He reached into his coat and produced a credstick. The gold plating caught the neon from the sign outside — OBI'S NOODLE HOUSE — and threw it in lazy arcs across the walls. He placed it on the desk between us. The denomination was stamped on the side. It was enough to keep me in cigarettes and rent for six months.

"I heard you're the best," he said. "I heard you've forgotten more about memory trading than most brokers will ever know."

I took a long drag. The smoke burned going down, which at least confirmed I was still alive.

"Who told you that?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters if I'm going to have to kill them."

He smiled, but it didn't reach his eyes. His eyes were the wrong color — too green, too bright, the kind of eyes that came from a biotech lab rather than a mother's womb. The rich in Kisima didn't just buy memories; they bought better eyeballs, better lungs, better whatever part of themselves they'd decided was defective.

"I need you to look at the memory," he said. "That's all. Look at it, tell me what it contains, and I'll be on my way."

"And if I don't like what I see?"

"Then we negotiate."

I picked up the slate. It was heavier than it should have been, weighted with the kind of security that suggested whoever had owned it didn't want it found. The encryption was custom, military-grade. I'd seen the patterns before, back when I wore a badge and carried a gun that fired real bullets.

"You know what happens to people who hold stolen memories in Kisima?" I said. "There's a man on Level 6 who peels the cortex like an onion. Layer by layer. He's very thorough. He charges by the hour."

"I'm aware of the risks."

"And you still want me to do this?"

"I still want you to do this."

I could have refused. I should have refused. The cigarette was half gone, the rain was picking up, and the tilapia smell was getting stronger as Obi's wife started the evening fry-up. Everything about this moment was wrong. The client was too nervous, the money was too much, and the memory on that slate was encrypted with a level of paranoia that suggested it contained something someone wanted very badly to forget.

I cracked it anyway.

The encryption was child's play once I got past the outer shell. The real work was in the inner layers, the ones designed to trap the unwary in recursive loops. I'd seen brokers lose their minds to those loops. They'd sit in their chairs, eyes open, drooling, trapped in the same second of memory replaying forever.

Ten minutes later, the screen went clear.

The memory began.

I was standing in a rain-slick street, the kind of narrow alley that ran between the mega-blocks like veins between organs. The neon was red and blue, the colors of a bruise. I knew this alley. I'd walked it a thousand times, back when I still wore the badge.

And there was me — younger, harder, with a haircut that said don't fuck with me and a coat that said I've already been fucked with enough.

I was talking to someone. A man. His face was shadowed, hidden under the hood of a coat that looked like the one my client was wearing now. The me in the memory was angry. I could see it in the set of my shoulders, the way my hands kept clenching and unclenching at my sides.

"You sold it," memory-me said. "You sold my past."

The hooded man said nothing.

"I gave you everything. My old cases, my old burns, the faces of everyone I ever put away. You promised — "

"I promised nothing."

"You promised you'd keep it safe."

The man laughed. It was a cold sound, metallic, like something grinding together in a machine that had long since forgotten how to be alive.

"Your past was never safe, Kali. It was never yours. You just didn't know who owned it."

The memory cut off. The screen went black.

I sat there for a long moment. The cigarette had burned down to the filter. I stubbed it out in the ashtray that was shaped like a lotus flower and had a crack running through its center.

"That's not possible," I said.

The client was watching me with those too-green eyes. "You recognize it."

"I don't recognize anything."

"You're lying."

"I'm a lot of things. Lying is a lifestyle choice."

He leaned forward. The chair groaned in protest. "That memory is from the night your partner died. The night you were yanked off the force. The night you became a broker instead of a cop."

"I said I don't — "

"You don't remember." He said it flat, without accusation. "That's the problem. That's why I came to you. You don't remember, and I need to know what you forgot."

The rain hammered the glass. The neon outside flickered — a dying tube in the sign across the street. Somewhere below, Obi's wife was yelling at a customer in rapid Swahili. The usual sounds. The usual city. But nothing was usual anymore.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"My name is not important."

"Then why should I help you?"

He reached into his coat again. This time, he pulled out something that made my blood run cold. It was a photograph, printed on actual paper, yellowed at the edges. Old-timer style. The picture showed two women standing in front of a noodle stall — this noodle stall, Obi's, back when the sign was hand-painted and the stools were made of wood rather than recycled plastic.

The women were me and my mother.

The picture was taken forty years ago.

My mother died when I was eight. I know this because I've known it my whole life. I have the memory of the funeral, the rain, the smell of mud and flowers. I have the memory of standing at the graveside in a dress that was too big for me, donated by someone who wanted to feel good about themselves.

But in this photograph, my mother looked alive. Healthy. And I looked — I looked older than eight. I looked like I was in my twenties.

I looked like I was looking at a woman who wasn't supposed to exist.

"Where did you get this?" My voice came out rough, cracked at the edges.

The client smiled. It wasn't a pleasant smile. It was the smile of a man who knew he'd just played the winning card in a game I didn't even know existed.

"The Archivist sends his regards," he said.

I lunged across the desk.

He was faster. He slipped sideways, out of the wobbling chair, and was on his feet before I could clear the desk. His coat whispered as he moved, the dry-weave rippling like liquid shadow. He reached the door and paused, one hand on the frame.

"Your past isn't missing, Kali," he said. "It was purchased. Stolen. Removed from you like a bad tooth."

"I'll kill you."

"You'll try." He tilted his head, those green eyes glinting in the neon wash. "But first, you'll find the Archivist. You'll find him because you have no choice. The memory on that slate is the key to everything you've forgotten. And everything you've forgotten is the key to everything you are."

He was gone before I could reach him. The door swung shut, and the rain continued its assault on the window, and the neon continued its flickering dance, and somewhere in the depths of Kisima, a man called the Archivist was waiting for me.

I looked down at the photograph in my hand.

My mother smiled up at me, frozen in forty-year-old paper, alive in a way I knew she couldn't be. Alive in a way that meant every memory I had was a lie.

The cigarette pack was empty.

The rain didn't stop.

Neither did I.

Chapter 3 — The Weight of Water

The photograph stayed in my hand long after the client's footsteps faded into the drumming rain. My mother's smile was small, almost shy, the way she'd smiled in the few real memories I had of her. But those memories were from a child's height, looking up. This woman in the photograph looked at me at eye level, and there was something in her expression I didn't recognize. Something careful. Something held back.

I'd seen that look before. On witnesses who were about to lie.

The rain kept coming. It always kept coming in Kisima. The city was built on a floodplain that had long since surrendered to the rising water, and the engineers had responded the way engineers always do — they built up instead of out, stacking floors upon floors until the megacity scraped the clouds and the streets became layers of steel and glass and desperate humanity. The rain was the one constant. It washed the streets clean, polished the neon, and erased the bloodstains faster than the cleaners could claim the work.

I put the photograph in my coat pocket. It was heavier than paper should be.

Obi's voice drifted up through the floorboards, muffled by the cheap insulation and the layers of grease that had accumulated over three decades of noodle steam. He was arguing with his wife about something — the price of fish, the rent, the usual. The sounds of normal life happening below me while my world turned inside out.

I went to the window. The neon across the street was still flickering, a dying sign advertising a club that had closed six months ago. The letters said CLUB LAZARUS, which was either a joke or a prophecy. In Kisima, you never knew which.

My reflection in the glass was a ghost — dark skin, darker circles under my eyes, hair cropped close to the skull in the way that made me look either ex-military or recently institutionalized. Both were accurate. The scar on my jaw caught the light, a pale line running from my ear to my chin. I'd gotten it before I lost the badge. I'd gotten it protecting someone who later testified against me.

That was the way of things. You give, you get nothing back. You trust, you get cut.

I pulled the slate from my desk drawer. The one the client had left behind. He'd given it to me to decrypt, but I'd cracked it deeper than he'd expected, and now the memory was mine. The question was what to do with it.

The alley in the memory was real. I'd walked it a thousand times. But the conversation that had happened there — the argument, the accusation, the hooded man who sold my past — that was blank. A hole in my consciousness where something important had been torn out.

I'd always had gaps. Everyone did. Memory was a fragile thing, easily corrupted, easily rewritten. But these weren't the normal gaps of an aging mind or a hard-lived life. These were surgical. Precise. The kind of removals that left no scar tissue, no trace of what had been there beyond the faint impression of absence.

Like a tongue probing the space where a tooth used to be.

I sat back down. The chair groaned. Everything in this office groaned, from the floorboards to the filing cabinet to me. The tilapia smell was getting stronger, mixing with the cigarette smoke that had soaked into the walls over the years. The place smelled like poverty and desperation, but it was mine.

I pulled up the city net on my terminal. The screen flickered, then stabilized into the familiar interface — a jungle of ads, alerts, and encrypted channels. I ignored the noise and went straight to the data I needed: the Registry of Cognitive Assets.

Every memory in Kisima was supposed to be registered. Every memory broker, every corporate memory lab, every black-market dealer who touched the stream of human experience had to file with the Registry. It was a law that had been passed after the Great Collapse, when the corporations had realized that memory was the most valuable resource they'd ever encountered — more valuable than oil, more valuable than data, more valuable than life itself. Because life was finite. Memory could be copied, sold, traded, and weaponized.

I typed in the parameters. Stolen memories, unsanctioned transfers, purchases of personal cognitive material. The Registry was supposed to be a wall. In practice, it was a sieve. But it was a sieve that left breadcrumbs.

The search returned nothing.

I tried again with different parameters — time period, location, the alley where the memory had taken place. Nothing. The system was either clean or cleaned.

That was when I knew this was bigger than one client with strange eyes and a dangerous smile. The Archivist had resources. He had reach. He'd erased my past from the official record, and he'd done it so thoroughly that even the Registry — which was supposed to be immutable — showed no trace of the theft.

I needed help. The kind of help that didn't come cheap and didn't come clean.

I made a call.

The connection took twelve seconds to route through the layers of encryption, bouncing off a dozen relay stations across the continent before settling on a face I hadn't seen in three years. The screen showed a woman with silver hair and a face like a knife's edge. She was sitting in what looked like a broom closet, but the shelves behind her were full of server racks so densely packed they seemed to glow with heat.

"Kali," she said. Her voice was flat, but there was something underneath it. Something that might have been concern. "You've been quiet."

"Never quiet, Odessa. Just selective."

"Selective." She almost smiled. "That's one word for it."

I held up the slate. "I need you to trace a purchase."

She squinted at the screen, adjusting something off-camera. "That's encrypted. Military-grade."

"I cracked it."

"Then you're better than you used to be."

"I had good teachers."

She snorted. "You had me. There's a difference." She leaned closer to her camera. "What's on it?"

"A memory. Mine. Stolen."

The silence that followed was heavy enough to carry across the connection. Odessa had been my partner in the early days, back before she'd gone full hermit and I'd gone full exile. She'd seen me at my worst, and she'd seen me at my best, and she knew what it meant when I said stolen.

"How much of it?" she asked.

"One night. The night everything went wrong."

"The night Merrick died."

I didn't respond. The name hung in the air like smoke.

"Kali," she said, and her voice was softer now. "I told you that night was a mess. I told you to walk away. You didn't listen."

"I remember."

"Do you? Or do you remember what they told you to remember?"

The question was like a blade slipped between my ribs. I'd come to Odessa for help, and she'd answered with a question I didn't want to ask myself.

"The client said the Archivist has my past," I said. "He said it was purchased. Removed. Like a bad tooth."

"The Archivist." She said the name like it tasted bad. "That's not a name you want in your mouth."

"You know him."

"I know of him. Everyone in the deep net knows of him. He's a collector. Not of objects — of identities. He buys lives, Kali. Complete packages. Memories, skills, muscle memory, even the little neural quirks that make a person who they are. He strips them from the source and installs them in a buyer who wants to be someone else."

"And my past is part of his collection."

"Apparently." She paused. "You should be careful. The Archivist doesn't collect anything he doesn't value. If he took your memories, it means you were someone worth being. That's dangerous."

"Everything's dangerous."

"This is a different kind of dangerous."

She was right. I knew she was right. But the photograph in my pocket was burning a hole through my conscience, and the memory on the slate was replaying in my mind — the younger me, the harder me, accusing a hooded man of selling my past.

I was the one who'd sold it, wasn't I? That's what the memory implied. I gave the man my old cases, my old burns, the faces of everyone I'd ever put away. I gave it to him willingly.

Why?

Odessa was still watching me through the screen, her silver hair catching the glow of her servers. "What are you going to do?"

"I'm going to find him."

"The Archivist?"

"No." I looked down at the photograph. My mother's smile, frozen in time. "The man in the memory. The one I gave everything to."

"And then?"

"Then I'm going to ask him what else I forgot."

The connection cut before she could respond. I sat in the darkness of my office, the rain hammering the window, the neon painting the walls in sickly colors, and I thought about holes. About absences. About the shape of a life that had been carved out of someone else's hands.

I thought about my mother, alive in a photograph that shouldn't exist.

I thought about everything I'd forgotten and everything I was about to remember.

The pack of cigarettes was empty. The ashtray was full. The city outside was a cathedral of neon and water, and somewhere in its depths, a man was waiting for me to come looking for answers I wasn't sure I wanted to find.

I stood up.

I put on my coat.

I went out into the rain.

Chapter 4 — The Weight of Rain

The rain hit me like a verdict.

Kisima never slept, but it did dream — and those dreams leaked through the city's neural conduits, painting the streets in colors that didn't exist in nature. Violet. Electric blue. A green that made my teeth ache. The wet asphalt reflected it all, turning the streets into rivers of liquid light, and I walked through them like a ghost haunting its own funeral.

The address on the slate was eleven blocks from my office. Eleven blocks through the meat of the city, past the open-air markets where vendors hawked stolen childhoods in glass vials, past the dream parlors with their flickering neon eyes, past the gutter preachers who screamed about the sanctity of the soul while selling their own congregations' prayers on the black market. Eleven blocks of noise and desperation and the constant, dripping weight of water that never stopped falling in this city.

I'd been in Kisima for fifteen years. I still wasn't used to the rain.

The building I stopped in front of was a corpse — a skeletal tower of concrete and rust that had been abandoned during the Great Collapse and never reclaimed. The windows were shattered, the entrance a gaping wound that led into darkness. But the slate hummed in my pocket, vibrating with proximity, and I knew that the man from my memory was somewhere inside.

I'd changed since the memory. My hands were older, scarred from years of work I couldn't remember doing. My body was harder in some places, softer in others, a testament to a life that had been partially erased. But the instinct was still there — the cop's instinct, the hunter's instinct, the thing that had kept me alive long enough to become a memory broker in a city that ate its children whole.

I went in.

The lobby was filled with the smell of mold and old blood. The elevators were dead, so I took the stairs — twelve flights of crumbling concrete and rusted railings, each step echoing in the darkness like a heartbeat. My coat was heavy with rain. My pistol was heavy with purpose. Neither was enough to stop the shaking in my hands.

The memory had shown me the man's face — or what I'd been able to see of it. A hood. A shadow. A voice that had been altered by technology until it sounded like gravel and static. But the slate had directions, and directions were better than faces.

I found the door on the seventh floor. It was new — steel reinforced, with a keypad that glowed green in the darkness. Someone had been spending money on this place. Someone had been preparing for company.

I didn't knock. I kicked the door in.

The room inside was a shrine.

Hundreds of screens lined the walls, each one displaying a different memory — a child's birthday party, a soldier's last moments, a woman's wedding day, a man's first kiss. They flickered and danced in the darkness, a constellation of stolen moments that made my skin crawl. In the center of the room, on a chair made of what looked like human vertebrae, sat the man from my memory.

He was older than I remembered. That was the first thing I noticed. His face was a roadmap of wrinkles and scars, and his eyes — his eyes were the color of old blood, watching me with a calm that made my trigger finger itch. He was wearing the same hood from the memory, but he'd pulled it back, revealing a skull that had been shaved clean and tattooed with circuitry.

"Kali," he said. His voice was smooth. Too smooth. "I've been expecting you."

"You have a name?"

"I have many." He smiled. "But you can call me Mwangi. It's the name I was born with, before the world decided I should be someone else."

"You sold my past."

"I didn't sell it." He stood up, and I saw that he was taller than I'd thought — a giant of a man, built like a mountain that had learned to walk. "I preserved it. There's a difference, though I understand why you might not see it."

"Explain it to me."

"Your memories weren't taken to hurt you. They were taken to protect you." He gestured to the screens around us. "I collect lives, Kali. I preserve them. The people who buy from me are looking for escape — from their own minds, their own pasts, their own failures. I give them a new skin to wear."

"And my skin was worth selling."

"Your skin was worth keeping." He stepped closer, and I didn't move. The pistol was in my hand, but I hadn't raised it. Something in his eyes told me I wouldn't need it. "You were a cop. You were a good cop, as far as cops go. But you were also a witness."

"To what?"

"To the night Kisima changed its mind."

The words hung in the air like a challenge. I'd heard rumors about the city's dream network — the massive neural grid that connected every citizen's subconscious, allowing corporations to harvest emotions, implant desires, and erase inconvenient truths. But I'd never heard anyone talk about it like this. Like it was alive.

"I was at the Merrick scene," I said. "I remember that much."

"You remember what they let you remember."

"And you know what I forgot."

"I know what you gave away." He reached into his coat and pulled out a small data chip, the kind that sold for a fortune on the black market. "I have it here. Your memories of that night — the real ones, the ones that were too dangerous to keep in your own head. You gave them to me for safekeeping. You were supposed to forget."

"Supposed to?"

"Something went wrong." His mouth twisted. "The Archivist got wind of my collection. He wanted your memories for himself. He paid me more than I could refuse. I sold him a copy — but I kept the original. I knew you'd come looking eventually."

"Then give it to me."

"Give it to you?" He laughed, and the sound was like rocks grinding together. "Kali, if I give you that chip, you'll remember everything. And I mean everything — the whole truth, not just the fragments you've been holding together with lies. Are you ready for that?"

I looked at the chip in his hand. It was small. It was light. It contained everything I'd lost — every face, every name, every moment I'd spent running from the truth. And I was terrified of it.

But I'd come this far.

"What else did I forget?" I asked.

Mwangi's smile faded. He looked at me for a long moment, his old blood eyes searching my face like he was looking for something he'd lost. Then he pressed the chip into my palm.

"Your mother," he said. "She's not dead. She's in the dream network — the deep layer, the part that's been corrupted by the Archivist's experiments. She's been there for twenty years, Kali. And she's been waiting for you to come find her."

Chapter 5 — The Mother in the Machine

The word hung in the stale, dead air of the shrine: *Mother.*

It didn’t compute. It was a null pointer, a broken link in the code of my identity. My mother was a faded holo-image in a cracked frame. A name on a tombstone I’d never visited in the overgrown cemetery district. A quiet, suffocating absence that had shaped me more than any presence ever had. She died. She had to. The story I’d told myself for twenty years was simple: sickness, then loss, then the hard, clean geometry of grief. It was a memory, or the ghost of one, solid enough to build a life upon.

Mwangi’s words shattered that geometry into a million jagged pieces.

“In the network,” I repeated. My voice was flat, a dead thing. The chip in my palm felt suddenly heavy, a tiny coffin of silicon holding a living ghost. “For twenty years.”

“Not living,” Mwangi clarified, his gravel-and-static voice softening to something almost gentle. It was worse than the smoothness. “Not in the way you and I are. Her body failed, yes. But her mind—her consciousness, her memories, the core of what she was—was uploaded. Preserved. A conscious mind in a cage of pure data.”

“Why? Who would—?”

“The Archivist.”

The name landed in the room like a third presence. A cold draft slithered through the broken windows, smelling of ozone and wet circuitry.

“Your mother wasn’t just anyone, Kali,” Mwangi said, turning back to the wall of flickering screens. He gestured, and one display flickered to life—a grainy security feed, old tech. It showed a laboratory, pristine white, filled with humming chrome monoliths. “Dr. Elara Soni. Chief Neuro-Architect of the Kisima Dream Initiative. She built the city’s neural network. The first version, anyway. The one that let us share dreams, before the corporations turned it into a marketplace for stolen souls.”

My mother. The quiet woman who hummed off-key while making tea, whose hands always smelled of soil from her small balcony garden, who read me stories about stars that weren’t drowned by the city’s perpetual twilight. She didn’t look like the woman in the grainy feed—a severe figure in a lab coat, her face a mask of focused intensity. But the eyes… I recognized the eyes. They were mine.

“She found something,” Mwangi continued. “A flaw. Or a feature, depending on who you ask. The deep layer of the network, the subconscious bedrock where dreams curdle into raw data… it was unstable. Prone to collapse, but also to… emergence. Things were growing down there. Sentient patterns. Memetic lifeforms feeding on the city’s accumulated psychic detritus. She wanted to shut it down, sanitize the deep layer. The corporations who funded the project—Merrick Corp, Primus Industries, all the old titans—wanted to exploit it. They saw a goldmine of unprecedented scale.”

“And my father?” The question was a reflex, a child’s plea for context.

Mwangi’s tattooed skull caught the light as he shook his head. “Gone. Another casualty of that war. He tried to help her. They both did. They were idealists. A dangerous thing to be in Kisima.” He faced me again. “She couldn’t shut it down. They had her cornered. So she did the next best thing. She gave herself to it. Anupload. A suicide that was also an infiltration. She became part of the deep layer, hoping to stabilize it from within. To fight the corruption at its source.”

The room spun. The walls of stolen memories blurred into a smear of light and shadow. I reached out, my hand finding the cold, rough concrete of the doorframe. My mother wasn’t dead. She was a prisoner in the city’s dreaming brain. A ghost in the machine.

And I had been chasing a ghost of my own. The man from the memory. The Archivist.

“His real name?” I asked. The words were chewed glass in my throat.

“He doesn’t have one anymore. He shed it like a skin. He was a project manager for Merrick. ambitious. Calculating. He saw what your mother built and saw not a network, but a kingdom. When she tried to stop it, he orchestrated the cover-up. The ‘accident’ that killed her. He erased the research, erased her, erased you and your father. He buried it all under layers of corporate secrecy and fabricated memories.”

He pointed to the data chip. “Your memories of that night? You saw him do it. You saw him give the order to have your father killed and your mother uploaded against her will. You were a kid, hiding in a vent. You saw everything. So he had your mind scrubbed, your identity rewritten. You became Kali, the orphan with no past. He even created a false history for you, complete with dead parents and a clean, if tragic, backstory.”

The pistol was in my hand now. I didn’t remember drawing it. The weight was a comfort, the only solid thing in a world that had liquefied.

“And you?” I asked. “Where do you fit in? The noble preserver of lives?”

A flicker of something—shame, maybe—crossed his scarred face. “I was a data recovery specialist for him, back then. Low-level. I was good at my job. I found the backdoor your mother left in the system. A key. I found you, too, years later. Working security, looking for ghosts you couldn’t name. I gave you a job. A purpose. I thought if I guided you, you’d find the truth and do what I was too cowardly to do: burn it all down.”

“And the chip?”

“A failsafe. Your memories of that night, the real ones, I pulled them from the deep layer years ago. They were degrading, caught in the static. I saved them. He found out. He’s been hunting me ever since.”

As if on cue, a sound sliced through the building’s silence. Not the rain. Not the wind. The sharp, rhythmic *whump-whump-whump* of mag-rotor blades.

Mwangi’s ancient eyes widened. “He’s here. He must have traced the chip’s activation.” He lunged for a console, his fingers flying across a keypad. One of the screens flickered to an external view of the building’s roof. A sleek, black Corporate Security skimmer was settling onto the crumbling concrete, its searchlight cutting a cone of white through the downpour.

“Time to go,” I said. The shock was still there, a cold stone in my gut, but the cop’s instinct was screaming louder now. It was a familiar, terrible symphony: the hunt, the hunted, the closing walls.

“No, you go,” Mwangi said. He pulled a heavy, rusted lever on the wall. A section of the floor groaned open, revealing a dark, vertical shaft lined with rusted pipes and cables. A service ladder descended into blackness. “I’ll slow them down. Buy you time.”

“You’re coming with me.”

“Kali, listen to me.” He grabbed my arm, his grip surprisingly strong. “That chip is your key. To your memories. To the network. To *her*. But he can’t get his hands on it. He can’t get his hands on you. Now go. The shaft connects to the old mag-lev maintenance tunnels. They run all through the undercity. You can get to the Nexus Spire from there.”

The sound of boots on the stairwell below, heavy and armored, echoed up to us. Mwangi was right. Arguing was a luxury we didn’t have.

I looked at him—the archivist, the coward, the man who’d haunted the edges of my life. He wasn’t the villain of this story. But he wasn’t the hero, either. He was just another man caught in the gears.

I nodded once. “The deep layer. How do I get in?”

He pointed at the chip. “That contains the access codes. Your mother’s codes. But be warned: the deep layer is not like the surface net. It’s raw. Primal. Your memories will be weapons and targets down there. And the Archivist… he’s been swimming in those depths for twenty years. He knows its currents better than anyone.”

The boots were on our floor now. A amplified voice boomed through the door, distorted by a speaker. “Mwangi Koryo! By mandate of the Kisima Corporate Authority, you are under arrest for data-trafficking and sedition! Surrender the asset and the memory-protocol!”

The asset. That was me.

I didn’t wait for a second invitation. I turned and descended into the dark. The ladder was cold and damp beneath my hands. The smell of rust and decay filled my nostrils. Above me, I heard Mwangi shout something defiant, followed by the percussive bark of a stun weapon and the heavy crash of the reinforced door finally giving way.

I didn’t look back. I climbed down, into the guts of the city, the data chip clutched in my fist like a grenade with the pin already pulled.

The maintenance tunnel at the bottom was a concrete throat, slick with moisture and illuminated by the sickly, intermittent glow of emergency strips. Water dripped from a thousand unseen points. The air was thick with the hum of the city’s buried heart.

I started walking, my footsteps echoing in the confined space. My reflection swam in the puddles on the floor—a hollow-eyed woman, holding a key to a past that might destroy her, walking toward a mother she’d mourned for two decades.

The rain never stopped. Now I knew why.

It was weeping for the ghosts in the machine.
