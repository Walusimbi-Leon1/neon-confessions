Chapter 2 — The Debt Collector

The smell of fried tilapia and burnt palm oil seeped through my floorboards like a persistent ghost. It was the sort of smell that got into your clothes, your hair, the soft tissue of your memories. I'd stopped noticing it three years ago, which was worse than noticing. You lose the texture of things, living above a noodle stall that never closes. The city outside my window hummed with the frequency of a bad tooth.

Rain sheeted down the reinforced glass in curtains of static.

The client sat across from me in the chair that wobbled unless you knew the exact angle to lean back. He didn't know the angle. He perched on the edge like a bird afraid of its own shadow, rain dripping from the hood of a coat that cost more than my rent. The coat was dry-weave, the kind that repels everything. Yet he shook. The wetness on his face wasn't rain.

"I need you to look at this," he said.

His voice had the nasal quality of someone who'd grown up in the upper stacks, where the air got thin and the acoustics bounced off glass. He held a data-slate like it might bite him.

"I'm not a courier service," I said. "If you want message drops, go to the pigeon man on Level 4. He charges by the gram and doesn't ask questions."

"This isn't a message." He slid the slate across my desk. The surface was scratched, the screen cracked in a spiderweb pattern from some impact. "It's a memory. Encrypted. I need you to decrypt it and tell me what it contains."

I lit a cigarette from the pack I kept in my desk drawer, the one with the faded label that promised something called "Menthol Paradise" that definitely was not paradise and probably wasn't menthol either. The smoke curled toward the ceiling fan, which spun with a lazy wobble.

"Memories aren't my trade anymore," I said. "I'm out of that business."

"That's not what I heard."

"Then you heard wrong."

He reached into his coat and produced a credstick. The gold plating caught the neon from the sign outside — OBI'S NOODLE HOUSE — and threw it in lazy arcs across the walls. He placed it on the desk between us. The denomination was stamped on the side. It was enough to keep me in cigarettes and rent for six months.

"I heard you're the best," he said. "I heard you've forgotten more about memory trading than most brokers will ever know."

I took a long drag. The smoke burned going down, which at least confirmed I was still alive.

"Who told you that?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters if I'm going to have to kill them."

He smiled, but it didn't reach his eyes. His eyes were the wrong color — too green, too bright, the kind of eyes that came from a biotech lab rather than a mother's womb. The rich in Kisima didn't just buy memories; they bought better eyeballs, better lungs, better whatever part of themselves they'd decided was defective.

"I need you to look at the memory," he said. "That's all. Look at it, tell me what it contains, and I'll be on my way."

"And if I don't like what I see?"

"Then we negotiate."

I picked up the slate. It was heavier than it should have been, weighted with the kind of security that suggested whoever had owned it didn't want it found. The encryption was custom, military-grade. I'd seen the patterns before, back when I wore a badge and carried a gun that fired real bullets.

"You know what happens to people who hold stolen memories in Kisima?" I said. "There's a man on Level 6 who peels the cortex like an onion. Layer by layer. He's very thorough. He charges by the hour."

"I'm aware of the risks."

"And you still want me to do this?"

"I still want you to do this."

I could have refused. I should have refused. The cigarette was half gone, the rain was picking up, and the tilapia smell was getting stronger as Obi's wife started the evening fry-up. Everything about this moment was wrong. The client was too nervous, the money was too much, and the memory on that slate was encrypted with a level of paranoia that suggested it contained something someone wanted very badly to forget.

I cracked it anyway.

The encryption was child's play once I got past the outer shell. The real work was in the inner layers, the ones designed to trap the unwary in recursive loops. I'd seen brokers lose their minds to those loops. They'd sit in their chairs, eyes open, drooling, trapped in the same second of memory replaying forever.

Ten minutes later, the screen went clear.

The memory began.

I was standing in a rain-slick street, the kind of narrow alley that ran between the mega-blocks like veins between organs. The neon was red and blue, the colors of a bruise. I knew this alley. I'd walked it a thousand times, back when I still wore the badge.

And there was me — younger, harder, with a haircut that said don't fuck with me and a coat that said I've already been fucked with enough.

I was talking to someone. A man. His face was shadowed, hidden under the hood of a coat that looked like the one my client was wearing now. The me in the memory was angry. I could see it in the set of my shoulders, the way my hands kept clenching and unclenching at my sides.

"You sold it," memory-me said. "You sold my past."

The hooded man said nothing.

"I gave you everything. My old cases, my old burns, the faces of everyone I ever put away. You promised — "

"I promised nothing."

"You promised you'd keep it safe."

The man laughed. It was a cold sound, metallic, like something grinding together in a machine that had long since forgotten how to be alive.

"Your past was never safe, Kali. It was never yours. You just didn't know who owned it."

The memory cut off. The screen went black.

I sat there for a long moment. The cigarette had burned down to the filter. I stubbed it out in the ashtray that was shaped like a lotus flower and had a crack running through its center.

"That's not possible," I said.

The client was watching me with those too-green eyes. "You recognize it."

"I don't recognize anything."

"You're lying."

"I'm a lot of things. Lying is a lifestyle choice."

He leaned forward. The chair groaned in protest. "That memory is from the night your partner died. The night you were yanked off the force. The night you became a broker instead of a cop."

"I said I don't — "

"You don't remember." He said it flat, without accusation. "That's the problem. That's why I came to you. You don't remember, and I need to know what you forgot."

The rain hammered the glass. The neon outside flickered — a dying tube in the sign across the street. Somewhere below, Obi's wife was yelling at a customer in rapid Swahili. The usual sounds. The usual city. But nothing was usual anymore.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"My name is not important."

"Then why should I help you?"

He reached into his coat again. This time, he pulled out something that made my blood run cold. It was a photograph, printed on actual paper, yellowed at the edges. Old-timer style. The picture showed two women standing in front of a noodle stall — this noodle stall, Obi's, back when the sign was hand-painted and the stools were made of wood rather than recycled plastic.

The women were me and my mother.

The picture was taken forty years ago.

My mother died when I was eight. I know this because I've known it my whole life. I have the memory of the funeral, the rain, the smell of mud and flowers. I have the memory of standing at the graveside in a dress that was too big for me, donated by someone who wanted to feel good about themselves.

But in this photograph, my mother looked alive. Healthy. And I looked — I looked older than eight. I looked like I was in my twenties.

I looked like I was looking at a woman who wasn't supposed to exist.

"Where did you get this?" My voice came out rough, cracked at the edges.

The client smiled. It wasn't a pleasant smile. It was the smile of a man who knew he'd just played the winning card in a game I didn't even know existed.

"The Archivist sends his regards," he said.

I lunged across the desk.

He was faster. He slipped sideways, out of the wobbling chair, and was on his feet before I could clear the desk. His coat whispered as he moved, the dry-weave rippling like liquid shadow. He reached the door and paused, one hand on the frame.

"Your past isn't missing, Kali," he said. "It was purchased. Stolen. Removed from you like a bad tooth."

"I'll kill you."

"You'll try." He tilted his head, those green eyes glinting in the neon wash. "But first, you'll find the Archivist. You'll find him because you have no choice. The memory on that slate is the key to everything you've forgotten. And everything you've forgotten is the key to everything you are."

He was gone before I could reach him. The door swung shut, and the rain continued its assault on the window, and the neon continued its flickering dance, and somewhere in the depths of Kisima, a man called the Archivist was waiting for me.

I looked down at the photograph in my hand.

My mother smiled up at me, frozen in forty-year-old paper, alive in a way I knew she couldn't be. Alive in a way that meant every memory I had was a lie.

The cigarette pack was empty.

The rain didn't stop.

Neither did I.
